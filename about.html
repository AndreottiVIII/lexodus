Data

All etymologies come from English Wiktionary, as extracted by Wiktextract and published on kaikki.org. Downloaded on 18 October 2026 (Wiktionary dump of 2 September 2026).

33 languages are read: Albanian, Arabic, Bulgarian, Catalan, Czech, Danish, Dutch, English, Estonian, Finnish, French, German, Greek, Hebrew, Hungarian, Icelandic, Italian, Latvian, Lithuanian, Maltese, Norwegian Bokmål, Persian, Polish, Portuguese, Romanian, Russian, Serbo-Croatian, Slovak, Slovene, Spanish, Swedish, Turkish, Ukrainian. Proper names and inflected forms are left out. A word counts once for each etymology.

Categories

borrowed
taken from another language (English pizza from Italian).
learned Latin
taken from written Latin, at any stage (Italian nazione).
inherited
passed down from an older language (Italian figlio from Latin filius). Not a borrowing.
calque
translated piece by piece (English bankrupt from Italian bancarotta).
unspecified
Wiktionary only says “from X”.

Borrowed, learned Latin and calque are shown at first; inherited and unspecified can be switched on.

Two levels

Each word is counted under the language it came from directly: Italian caffè under Ottoman Turkish. Opening a row shows the step before, for the same words: Ottoman Turkish had caffè from Arabic. The step before counts the same category as the row: under “Ottoman Turkish · borrowed” only the borrowed words. Steps inside one language (Middle English to English) are skipped. All Latin varieties are counted as one “Latin” unless you split them.

The grid and the words

The grid on the first page has one row for each of the 33 languages (the one that took the words) and one column for each language it took them from, plus “Latin” (all varieties) and “other” (every other language). It counts borrowed, learned Latin and calque. The second level counts the language one step before the direct one.

Each number on a language page opens the list of its words, with the meaning (the first English definition in Wiktionary, cut at 120 characters) and where each word came from. Each word opens a page with its way, step by step, the start of its Wiktionary etymology and the languages that took it afterwards. Words with two or more possible origins are not in the lists; their page shows the possibilities side by side.

Limits

  • The numbers are a snapshot of Wiktionary, not a measure of the vocabulary. Many entries have no etymology: about half of the Italian ones, nine in ten of the Maltese ones.
  • Coverage is good when 70% or more of a language's entries have an etymology, fair between 30% and 70%, poor below 30%.
  • Editors of different languages write differently (Romanian almost always says “borrowed”), so numbers from two languages are not directly comparable.
  • No dates: Wiktionary rarely says when a word arrived.
  • Families, proto-languages and “Translingual” (scientific names) are hidden by default.
