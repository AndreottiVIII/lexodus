How lexodus was made

lexodus shows which languages a language took words from, and which languages took words from it. It reads the etymologies written by the volunteers of English Wiktionary. It does not use any other dictionary, and it does not try to correct them.

Where the data comes from

All entries come from English Wiktionary, in the machine-readable form produced by Wiktextract and published on kaikki.org. The file was downloaded on 18 October 2026 (Wiktionary dump of 2 September 2026).

We read 33 languages of Europe and its neighbours: Italian, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Catalan, Romanian, English, German, Dutch, Swedish, Danish, Norwegian Bokmål, Icelandic, Polish, Czech, Slovak, Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Serbo-Croatian, Slovene, Greek, Hungarian, Finnish, Estonian, Lithuanian, Latvian, Albanian, Maltese, Turkish, Arabic, Persian and Hebrew. The languages words came from can be any language Wiktionary names.

Only dictionary words count: proper names and inflected forms (plurals, verb forms) are left out. A word is counted once for each of its etymologies, not once for each part of speech.

What the categories mean

Borrowed
Wiktionary says the word was taken from another language, for example English pizza from Italian.
Learned Latin
The word was taken from written Latin, at any stage (classical, late, medieval, modern), for example Italian nazione from Latin nātiōnem. We count every borrowing from Latin here, so that it is not confused with words that simply came down from Latin.
Inherited
The word was passed down from an older language, for example Italian figlio from Latin filius. This is not borrowing. Steps inside the same language (Middle English to English, Ottoman Turkish to Turkish) are not shown.
Calque
The word was translated piece by piece from another language, for example English bankrupt from Italian bancarotta (English bankrupt from Italian banca rotta.ldquo;broken benchEnglish bankrupt from Italian banca rotta.rdquo;).
Unspecified
Wiktionary only says “from X”, without saying whether the word was borrowed or inherited. These are many: for Italian there are almost as many as borrowings and learned Latin words together.

A word is linked to the language it came from directly. Italian caffè came from Turkish, which took it from Arabic: here it counts for Turkish only.

What the numbers cannot tell

  • They are a snapshot of Wiktionary, not a measure of the vocabulary. Many entries have no etymology at all: about half of the Italian ones, nine in ten of the Maltese ones. Each language page shows this share.
  • Coverage is good when 70% or more of a language's entries have an etymology, fair between 30% and 70%, poor below 30%.
  • Editors write differently. In some languages they almost always write “borrowed from”, in others “from”. Romanian is the clearest case: its borrowings from French look huge partly for this reason. Two numbers from two different languages are not directly comparable.
  • Uncertain words are the ones whose etymology says “possibly”, “probably”, “uncertain” or gives two possible sources. They are counted, and shown apart.
  • No dates. Wiktionary rarely says when a word arrived, and the few dates it gives are often about something else. lexodus does not show when words moved.
  • Not every label is a language. Wiktionary also names families (“Germanic”), reconstructed proto-languages (“Proto-Slavic”) and “Translingual”, which holds scientific names. They are hidden by default; a switch shows them.
